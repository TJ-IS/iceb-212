# Not All GenAI Disclosures Are Alike

## How the Stage of GenAI Involvement Shapes Purchase Intention for Digital Games

**ICEB 2026 · Beijing, China**

<!--
Presenter notes: (30 seconds)
Good morning everyone. My name is Fangzhan Lin, and today I will present our study, "Not All GenAI Disclosures Are Alike." The paper examines how disclosure of generative AI involvement shapes players' purchase intention for digital games. The key idea is that disclosure is not just a simple warning label. In games, it can also signal new forms of interaction and novelty, depending on how the involvement of GenAI is described.
-->

# INTRODUCTION

<div class="narrative-grid">
<div>
<h3>GenAI use is becoming visible</h3>
<p>Players increasingly encounter explicit statements that a game uses GenAI in product descriptions, development notes, or platform labels.</p>
</div>
<div>
<h3>Disclosure can hurt valuation</h3>
<p>Prior work on AI-generated products suggests that disclosure can weaken source-related beliefs tied to human creation.</p>
</div>
<div>
<h3>Games complicate the prediction</h3>
<p>Games are interactive hedonic systems, so players evaluate not only how a game was made, but also what it may do during play.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="slide-bottom-claim">
The key issue is whether GenAI disclosure is interpreted as a production cue, an experiential cue, or both.
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (45 seconds)
Let me start with the motivation. GenAI use is becoming increasingly visible in digital games, for example in product descriptions, developer notes, or platform labels. Prior research on AI-generated products often suggests a negative reaction: when people know that AI is involved, they may infer less human labor, less craft, and lower authenticity. But games complicate this prediction. A game is not only a finished creative product; it is also an interactive hedonic system. Players care about how the game was made, but also about what the game can do during play. So the same disclosure may work as both a production cue and an experiential cue.
-->

# Research Question

**How does GenAI disclosure influence players' purchase intention for digital games, and through what mechanisms does this effect emerge?**

<!--
Presenter notes: (30 seconds)
This leads to our research question: how does GenAI disclosure influence players' purchase intention for digital games, and through what mechanisms does this effect emerge? We focus on two belief-based mechanisms. One is perceived effort, which captures the human labor and craft that players attribute to the game. The other is perceived novelty, which captures whether the game seems fresh, technologically distinctive, and potentially different from existing games.

# CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND

<div class="tension-board">
<div>
<span>Source-related inference</span>
<h3>AI disclosure may reduce perceived human effort</h3>
<p>Creative products are often valued partly because they are believed to embody human labor, intentionality, and craft.</p>
</div>
<div>
<span>Experience-related inference</span>
<h3>AI disclosure may increase perceived novelty</h3>
<p>In games, GenAI can also signal adaptive dialogue, event variation, and responsive world behavior during play.</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="slide-bottom-claim">
This tension motivates a dual-pathway framework rather than a one-directional disclosure effect.
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (60 seconds)
The conceptual background is built around a tension. On the one hand, AI disclosure can reduce source-related value. For many creative products, people value the object partly because they believe it contains human effort, intention, and craft. If GenAI is disclosed, that belief may be weakened. On the other hand, games are experience-oriented and interactive. In this context, GenAI may also signal adaptive dialogue, dynamic events, or responsive world behavior. That can make the game seem more novel. So instead of assuming one overall negative disclosure effect, we propose a dual-pathway framework: a negative pathway through lower perceived effort and a positive pathway through higher perceived novelty.
-->

# Stage of GenAI Involvement

<p class="section-kicker">CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND</p>

<div class="stage-compare">
<div>
<h3>Pre-interaction involvement</h3>
<p>GenAI is described as helping create dialogue, story events, and world content before play.</p>
<strong>Likely interpretation:</strong>
<span>A backstage production aid.</span>
</div>
<div>
<h3>During-interaction involvement</h3>
<p>GenAI is described as generating dialogue, in-game events, and world content in real time during gameplay.</p>
<strong>Likely interpretation:</strong>
<span>An in-play interaction capability.</span>
</div>
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (55 seconds)
The next key distinction is the stage of GenAI involvement. We distinguish pre-interaction involvement from during-interaction involvement. In the pre-interaction condition, GenAI is described as helping create content before play starts, such as dialogue, story events, and world content. This sounds more like a backstage production aid. In the during-interaction condition, GenAI is described as generating content in real time during gameplay. This sounds more like an in-play interaction capability. Our argument is that this distinction should matter especially for novelty. During-interaction involvement is more likely to make players imagine a dynamic and responsive experience.
-->

<!--

This figure summarizes the research model. GenAI disclosure affects two perceptions. First, it can lower perceived effort. Second, it can increase perceived novelty. The stage of GenAI involvement moderates the disclosure-to-novelty path, because novelty should be more strongly activated when GenAI is framed as part of gameplay rather than only part of production. # RESEARCH METHOD

<div class="method-snapshot">
<div><strong>Design</strong><span>Scenario-based online experiment in digital games.</span></div>
<div><strong>Conditions</strong><span>No disclosure, pre-interaction disclosure, during-interaction disclosure.</span></div>
<div><strong>Sample</strong><span>399 valid responses; 133 participants per condition.</span></div>
</div>

<div class="metric-row metric-row-tight">
<span><strong>3</strong><br/>conditions</span>
<span><strong>399</strong><br/>responses</span>
<span><strong>133</strong><br/>per cell</span>
<span><strong>5,000</strong><br/>bootstrap resamples</span>
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (45 seconds)
To test the model, we conducted a scenario-based online experiment in the context of digital games. The design had three conditions: no disclosure, pre-interaction disclosure, and during-interaction disclosure. We collected 399 valid responses, with 133 participants in each condition. In the analysis, we used mean comparisons, regression models with controls, and bootstrap mediation with 5,000 resamples. This design allows us to isolate the wording of the GenAI disclosure while keeping the focal product constant.
-->

# Stimulus and Procedure

<div class="procedure-lane">
<div><span>01</span><strong>Scenario</strong><p>Participants imagined browsing a digital game platform.</p></div>
<div><span>02</span><strong>Product page</strong><p>They viewed a premium narrative role-playing game.</p></div>
<div><span>03</span><strong>Disclosure</strong><p>The GenAI statement varied across three conditions.</p></div>
</div>

<div class="stimulus-band">
<div>
<span>Held constant</span>
<strong>Title, genre, price, screenshots, and product description.</strong>
</div>
<div>
<span>Manipulated</span>
<strong>No disclosure, pre-interaction involvement, or during-interaction involvement.</strong>
</div>
</div>

<div class="slide-bottom-claim compact">
The manipulation changes the disclosed stage of GenAI contribution, not the timing of disclosure presentation.
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (55 seconds)
The procedure was straightforward. Participants imagined browsing a digital game platform. They then viewed a product page for a premium narrative role-playing game. Across conditions, the title, genre, price, screenshots, and product description were held constant. What changed was only the GenAI disclosure statement. In one condition, there was no AI statement. In the pre-interaction condition, GenAI was described as helping before gameplay. In the during-interaction condition, GenAI was described as contributing during gameplay. Importantly, this manipulation changes the disclosed stage of contribution, not the timing of disclosure presentation.
-->

# RESULTS: The Effort Pathway

<p class="section-kicker">H1, H2: source-related negative mechanism</p>

<div class="evidence-route effort-route">
<div class="route-main">
<span>H1</span>
<h3>Disclosure lowered perceived effort</h3>
<p>Perceived effort was significantly lower in the disclosure conditions than in the no-disclosure condition.</p>
<strong>Welch's t(264.6) = -6.18, p &lt; .001, d = -0.60</strong>
</div>
<div class="route-side">
<span>H2</span>
<h3>Effort still mattered for purchase intention</h3>
<p>Perceived effort positively predicted purchase intention, although the indirect effect through effort was marginal.</p>
<strong>r = .216, p &lt; .001; indirect p = .053</strong>
</div>
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (55 seconds)
Now I turn to the effort pathway, corresponding to H1 and H2. H1 was supported: GenAI disclosure lowered perceived effort compared with no disclosure. The effect was statistically significant, with Welch's t equal to -6.18 and p below .001. H2 was also supported: perceived effort positively predicted purchase intention. However, the indirect effect through effort was only marginal, with p equal to .053. So the negative source-related mechanism exists, but in this setting it is not the strongest pathway to purchase intention.
-->

# RESULTS: The Novelty Pathway

<p class="section-kicker">H3, H5: positive mechanism</p>

<div class="evidence-route novelty-route">
<div class="route-main">
<span>H3</span>
<h3>Disclosure increased perceived novelty</h3>
<p>GenAI disclosure made the focal game appear more technologically distinctive relative to no disclosure.</p>
<strong>Welch's t(251.0) = 2.88, p = .004, d = 0.32</strong>
</div>
<div class="route-side">
<span>H5</span>
<h3>Novelty strongly predicted purchase intention</h3>
<p>Perceived novelty showed a much stronger link to purchase intention than perceived effort.</p>
<strong>r = .554, p &lt; .001; beta = 0.409, p &lt; .001</strong>
</div>
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (55 seconds)
The novelty pathway tells the other side of the story. H3 was supported: GenAI disclosure increased perceived novelty relative to no disclosure. The effect was significant, with Welch's t equal to 2.88 and p equal to .004. H5 was also strongly supported: perceived novelty had a much stronger relationship with purchase intention than perceived effort, with r equal to .554 and regression beta equal to .409. This suggests that in digital games, GenAI disclosure can activate a positive technology-based inference, not only a negative production-based inference.
-->

# RESULTS: Stage-Contingent Novelty

<p class="section-kicker">H4: the positive pathway depends on the disclosed stage of involvement</p>

<div class="stage-spotlight">
<div class="spotlight-main">
<span>During-interaction</span>
<h3>During-interaction disclosure produced higher novelty</h3>
<p>Novelty was significantly higher when GenAI was framed as contributing during gameplay rather than before gameplay.</p>
<strong>Welch's t(263.8) = 2.68, p = .008, d = 0.33</strong>
</div>
<div class="spotlight-side">
<span>Moderated mediation</span>
<h3>The indirect novelty path emerged mainly during gameplay</h3>
<p>The positive indirect effect through novelty was significant for during-interaction disclosure, but not for pre-interaction disclosure.</p>
<strong>Index = 0.100, 95% CI [0.017, 0.197]</strong>
</div>
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (65 seconds)
The next result is central to the paper. H4 asks whether the novelty pathway depends on the stage of GenAI involvement. The answer is yes. Novelty was significantly higher when GenAI was framed as contributing during gameplay rather than before gameplay. The Welch's t value was 2.68, with p equal to .008. The moderated mediation result points in the same direction: the indirect effect through novelty was significant mainly for the during-interaction disclosure condition, and the index of moderated mediation was positive with a confidence interval excluding zero. So the positive effect of disclosure is not automatic. It becomes stronger when disclosure helps players imagine a novel gameplay experience.
-->

# DISCUSSION

<div class="takeaway-stairs">
<div><strong>1</strong><span>GenAI disclosure is not a uniform negative cue.</span></div>
<div><strong>2</strong><span>It simultaneously weakens perceived human effort and strengthens perceived novelty.</span></div>
<div><strong>3</strong><span>The positive novelty route is strongest when GenAI is framed as part of gameplay.</span></div>
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (55 seconds)
The overall message is that GenAI disclosure is not a uniform negative cue. It does reduce perceived human effort, which can hurt purchase intention. But it also increases perceived novelty, which can improve purchase intention. More importantly, the positive route is strongest when GenAI is framed as part of gameplay. This helps explain why consumers may react differently to different GenAI disclosures. They are not only responding to the presence of AI. They are also interpreting what kind of role AI is expected to play.
-->

# Practical Implications

<div class="playbook-grid">
<div><span>Avoid</span><strong>Do not treat all GenAI disclosures as equivalent.</strong></div>
<div><span>Clarify</span><strong>Explain whether GenAI contributes to production or to gameplay experience.</strong></div>
<div><span>Emphasize</span><strong>When appropriate, foreground in-play value rather than only backstage automation.</strong></div>
</div>

<div class="slide-bottom-claim compact">
Disclosure wording is a design lever: it can either highlight reduced human input or activate expectations of a novel play experience.
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (50 seconds)
The practical implication is that firms should not treat all GenAI disclosures as equivalent. Disclosure wording matters. If GenAI is described only as backstage automation, players may focus on reduced human input. But if GenAI is accurately described as supporting in-play value, such as dynamic dialogue or responsive events, it may activate expectations of novelty. The point is not to exaggerate AI capabilities. Rather, the point is to make the role of GenAI clear so that players can form the right expectation about the product experience.
-->

# Limitations and Future Research

<div class="limitation-lane">
<div><span>Beliefs</span><strong>Other perceptions remain open.</strong><p>Future work can examine perceived quality, authenticity, and realism.</p></div>
<div><span>Scope</span><strong>Single genre and product-page scenario.</strong><p>Future studies can test other game genres and disclosure contexts.</p></div>
<div><span>Moderators</span><strong>Individual differences may matter.</strong><p>AI attitudes and innovation orientation may further condition reactions.</p></div>
</div>

<div class="slide-bottom-claim compact">
Together, these extensions would test the boundary conditions of the dual-pathway account.
</div>

<!--
Presenter notes: (45 seconds)
There are also several limitations. First, the study focuses on the stage of disclosed GenAI involvement and does not examine other potentially important perceptions, such as quality, authenticity, or realism. Second, the experiment uses a single game genre and a single product-page scenario, which may limit generalizability. Third, although we included several controls, future research could examine whether individual differences in AI attitudes or innovation orientation further condition the observed effects.
-->
